After the Second World War, as the scale of Nazi atrocities became clear, one question kept surfacing: how did ordinary people do this? Were Germans somehow uniquely obedient, or was something more general at work? Stanley Milgram, a psychologist at Yale, built an experiment in the early 1960s to find out.

The experiments were conducted half a world away, but they describe something I recognize. Bangladeshi life is organized around authority relationships in families, in workplaces, in politics, and Milgram's findings land differently when you've grown up inside that.

## The Experiment

The setup was simple and quietly brutal. Participants, ordinary Americans from a range of backgrounds, were told they were in a study on learning and memory. Each was made the "teacher" and told to administer increasingly powerful electric shocks to a "learner" (actually an actor) whenever he made a mistake on a memory test.

The shock generator was fake but convincing, with switches running from 15 volts ("Slight Shock") to 450 volts ("XXX Danger: Severe Shock"). A researcher in a lab coat stood by and told participants to continue. The learner, hidden from view but audible, would pound on the wall as the voltage climbed, complain about a heart condition, and then, after 330 volts, go silent.

When participants hesitated, the experimenter had four lines, delivered in order:

- "Please continue."
- "The experiment requires that you continue."
- "It is absolutely essential that you continue."
- "You have no other choice; you must go on."

## The Findings

Milgram asked psychiatrists to predict the results beforehand. They estimated that about 1% of participants would go to the maximum.

Sixty-five percent went to 450 volts.

Most of them showed obvious distress (sweating, trembling, nervous laughter) and continued anyway. That detail matters more than the headline number. These weren't people who didn't care. They were people who cared and complied regardless.

The variations are where it gets specific. Put the learner in the same room and obedience fell to 40%. Move the study from Yale to a downtown office and it fell to 48%. Have other "teachers" refuse first, and it collapsed to 10%.

That last figure is the one I hold onto. Obedience isn't a fixed human constant. It depends heavily on whether anyone else in the room has already said no.

## Reading This From Here

Bangladeshi society runs on clearly defined hierarchies: the *boro-choto* dynamic in families, the deference owed to teachers, government officials, religious leaders. Milgram's results aren't a foreign curiosity in that context.

The cultural pressure is real and it stacks. Where collective harmony outweighs individual dissent, questioning authority is already costly, and *manush ki bolbe*, what will people say, adds a second layer on top of whatever the authority figure himself is demanding. In Milgram's terms, we have more than one experimenter in the room.

But I don't think the answer is importing a wholesale suspicion of authority. Bangladesh's communal structures also show what functioning authority does well. *Samaj*, our tradition of collective problem-solving, is often more effective against an abusive official than isolated individual resistance would be, which is close to what Milgram's peer-refusal variation found. Many practices that look restrictive from outside turn out to have a reason worth understanding before discarding.

What I've settled on is narrower: question without disrespect, and know your limits in advance. Our culture places high value on respect, especially toward elders, and I've found you can maintain *adab* while still asking what a directive actually requires you to do. And in a workplace where a junior employee won't contradict a superior (a common enough setup here, with corruption pressures attached), deciding your ethical boundary before you're standing at the console is most of the work. Milgram's participants weren't given time to think. That was the point.

## Where This Bites

In professional settings, deference to superiors produces situations structurally close to the experiment, where an unethical instruction goes unchallenged because challenging it isn't done.

In politics, our history shows both sides. The Language Movement and the student protests that followed it are a record of questioning authority shaping the country. So is what happens when nobody does.

In families, particularly urban ones, the shift from absolute parental authority toward negotiation over education and marriage is visible within a single generation.

## What I'm Left With

If Milgram had run this in Dhaka instead of New Haven, would the numbers have changed? Probably at the margins. The specific form deference takes here is ours; the underlying tendency looks like it belongs to everyone.

The finding I keep returning to isn't the 65%. It's the 10%. Obedience collapsed once one other person refused first. Whatever else that says, it says the first person to object is doing something disproportionate to the effort it costs them.
