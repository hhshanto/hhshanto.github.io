Stoicism is a Greek school of philosophy founded by Zeno of Citium around 300 BCE, and it has outlasted almost everything from its era. The reason is unglamorous: it was built to be used. The Stoics were less interested in constructing a system than in answering a practical question: how do you stay steady when things go wrong, which they will.

Its best-known figures wrote accordingly. Seneca wrote letters. Epictetus was a former slave whose lectures were transcribed by a student. Marcus Aurelius was an emperor writing a private notebook he never intended anyone to read. None of them were producing philosophy for other philosophers.

## The Four Virtues

The Stoics organized the good life around four qualities. **Wisdom** is discerning right from wrong through clear judgment. **Courage** is holding to what's right when it costs you. **Justice** is acting fairly toward others and weighting the common good. **Temperance** is moderation and self-control.

The claim underneath these is stronger than it looks: that virtue is sufficient for a good life, and that health, money, and reputation are worth having but not worth your peace of mind. That's the part most modern readers quietly skip.

## The Dichotomy of Control

This is the central move, and everything else follows from it. Some things are up to you: your judgments, choices, and actions. Most things are not: other people's opinions, outcomes, the weather, your body, the past.

Almost all unnecessary suffering comes from treating the second category as though it were the first.

The practical version: if you're anxious about a work project, the anxiety is usually attached to your manager's reaction, which you don't control. Move the effort to the part you do control, the quality of what you produce, and the anxiety has less to attach to. This isn't a trick for feeling better. It's a claim that you were misallocating attention.

## Memento Mori

*Memento mori* means "remember you must die." It reads as morbid and isn't meant to be. The point is that a finite life makes its contents matter more, not less. Petty conflicts shrink when measured against the actual amount of time available. Seneca's version is blunter than most: we aren't given a short life, we make it short by wasting it.

## Amor Fati

*Amor fati* is "love of fate": not merely accepting what happens but treating it as material. Setbacks stop being interruptions to your life and become the thing you're working with.

This is the doctrine most easily turned into nonsense. Taken loosely it becomes a justification for accepting things that should be resisted. The Stoics were not quietists; Marcus ran an empire and Cato died opposing Caesar. The acceptance is of what has already happened, not of what you could still change, which puts you right back at the dichotomy of control.

## Building a Practice

The daily version is fairly plain. Spend a few minutes observing your own thoughts, which is how you catch an irrational worry before it compounds. Occasionally imagine losing something you value. Negative visualization tempers wanting and sharpens gratitude for what's currently there. Keep a journal: what set you off, what part of it you controlled, what you'd do differently. End the day listing what went well.

Then evaluate the day's actions against the four virtues. That last step is the one that separates Stoicism from a stress management routine, and it's the one most often dropped.

## A Caveat

Stoicism is currently fashionable, and the fashionable version is thin: productivity advice with Latin attached. The full doctrine asks considerably more than the self-help extract does, including the uncomfortable claim that external goods don't determine whether your life went well. Whether you accept that is a real question. It's worth meeting the actual argument before deciding.
